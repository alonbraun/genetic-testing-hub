The single-cell multiomics market is on a steep upward trajectory, with new projections from SNS Insider placing its value at $18.20 billion by 2035. The forecast, released today, underscores how rapidly this field has moved from a niche research methodology into a central pillar of genomics infrastructure — with implications that stretch from oncology diagnostics to drug development and precision medicine workflows.

## The Technology Driving the Surge

Single-cell multiomics refers to the simultaneous profiling of multiple molecular layers — genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, and proteomics — within individual cells rather than averaging signals across bulk tissue. This granularity allows researchers and clinicians to detect rare cell populations, map tumor heterogeneity with far greater resolution, and understand how gene regulatory networks behave at the single-cell level. The convergence of next-generation sequencing platforms, advanced microfluidics, and increasingly powerful bioinformatics pipelines has made these assays more accessible and cost-effective than they were just a few years ago. As instrument throughput increases and reagent costs continue to decline, laboratories that previously could only consider bulk RNA sequencing are now evaluating single-cell and multimodal approaches as viable routine tools.

## Clinical and Commercial Implications

The projected market size reflects more than academic interest — it signals genuine commercial momentum across diagnostics, biopharma R&D, and translational medicine. In oncology, single-cell multiomics is beginning to inform biomarker discovery and companion diagnostic development, offering a more complete picture of the tumor microenvironment than any single-modality assay can provide. Liquid biopsy developers are also watching this space closely, as multiomics applied to circulating tumor cells could dramatically improve sensitivity and specificity for early cancer detection. Pharmaceutical companies investing in cell and gene therapies depend on this level of cellular resolution to characterize manufacturing outputs and patient responses. The cumulative demand from these sectors is what underpins the multi-billion-dollar growth forecast.

## What's Next for the Industry

As the market expands, competition among platform providers will intensify around throughput, ease of use, and data integration capabilities. Standardization will become a critical industry challenge — reproducibility across laboratories and regulatory acceptance of multiomics-derived biomarkers will require consensus frameworks that the field has not yet fully established. Investment in bioinformatics infrastructure will be as important as investment in wet-lab instrumentation, given the enormous data volumes these assays generate. Reimbursement pathways for clinically applied multiomics panels will also need to evolve in step with the science.

For the genetic testing industry, the maturation of single-cell multiomics represents a structural shift in what a genomic assay is capable of delivering — one that will redefine the standard of evidence for precision medicine decisions over the coming decade.
